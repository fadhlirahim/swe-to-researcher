Overview & philosophy

Most people fail this not for lack of resources — there are too many — but because they learn in the wrong shape. The philosophy below matters more than the curriculum.

The honest premise

  • Mastery is years, not months. Be suspicious of anyone selling "AI researcher in 8 weeks" — including this roadmap. It's a map, not a contract.
  • But useful competence is months. "Build a GPT from scratch and read most papers" in ~3–6 months; "run a clean extension of a published result" in ~6–12.
  • "Researcher" is a ladder, not a binary. You climb it by shipping artifacts, not finishing courses.
  • The bottleneck is reps and taste, not information. Everything you need is free. What's scarce is the discipline to build, reproduce, and write — repeatedly, in public.

The 5 principles

1 · Build first, theory just-in-time.

Don't spend 6 months on linear algebra before touching a model. Build, hit a wall, learn exactly that wall's theory, continue.

2 · Spiral, don't sequence.

The phases are a competence map, not a strict order. A vertical slice early beats a perfect horizontal foundation.

3 · Artifacts over courses.

A finished course is worth nothing; a reproduced result with a writeup is worth a lot. Every phase ends in something shippable.

4 · Reproduce before you innovate.

You earn the right to have ideas by first reproducing others'. 80% of the real learning lives here.

5 · Learn in public.

A private learner is invisible with no feedback loop. Push to GitHub, write notes, join a community. This is the feedback mechanism and the network — not vanity.

The cardinal sin of empirical ML is self-deception — believing a result that isn't real because you wanted it to be. Almost everything in the research-method section is a defense against that one failure.

0

Orientation

≈ 1 week Kill the "where do I start" paralysis by shipping a trained model in your first days. You're a strong engineer — the trap isn't capability, it's spending three weeks "preparing to learn." Don't.

Do this, in order

  • Environment (½ day) — PyTorch; a consumer GPU or free Colab/Kaggle. Don't over-build it.
  • First vertical slice (1–2 days) — run fast.ai Lesson 1 or train nanoGPT on tiny-shakespeare. Watch a loss curve drop. You won't understand most of it — that's fine.
  • Public scaffolding (½ day) — a GitHub repo, a LOG.md research log (the single highest-ROI habit here), a paper-notes file.
  • Join one community — the EleutherAI Discord. Lurk.
  • Set your target — write "my 6-month target is L2" in your log.

Milestone

  • A loss curve you produced, in your repo.
  • First LOG.md entry written.
  • First paper skimmed figures-first (try TinyStories) + 3-sentence note.
  • EleutherAI joined.
Traps — tooling rabbit holes; "I need to understand it first" (no: run it, then understand it); skipping the log.

1

Foundations

≈ 4–8 weeks Understand and implement from a blank file the machinery under every model: backprop, gradient descent, a neural net, core ML. Your gap isn't coding — it's the intuition for why nets train and the ML grammar that makes experiments interpretable.

What to learn (priority order)

  • Backpropagation — the chain rule on a computation graph. The single most important thing here. Implement reverse-mode autodiff and most of DL stops being magic.
  • Gradient descent & optimizers — SGD, momentum, Adam; learning rate as the thing that matters most.
  • A neural net from scratch — MLP, activations, initialization.
  • Core ML — cross-entropy, train/val/test, overfitting, regularization, bias–variance. The grammar of every experiment.
  • Math, just-in-time — linear algebra, the chain rule, probability/cross-entropy/KL. Learn each when a model forces you to.

Primary path

Karpathy — Neural Networks: Zero to Hero is the spine. Do the exercises. Build micrograd (a tiny autodiff engine — this is backprop) and makemore (a char-level LM). One breadth reference: fast.ai or d2l.ai. Math lookup only: Mathematics for ML.

Deliverable — reimplement micrograd + makemore from memory (not copy-paste), pushed with READMEs. Bonus: a "what backprop actually computes" explainer — teaching it finds your holes.

Milestone — you've finished when you can…

  • Implement reverse-mode autodiff from a blank file; hand-derive one gradient to check it.
  • Build, train, and debug an MLP without copying (diagnose a bad LR from the curve).
  • Explain cross-entropy, the val split, overfitting + 3 fixes — in your own words.
  • Read an empirical ML paper and follow its training setup.
Traps — watching instead of building (close the video, rebuild from a blank file); math-first detours; copy-paste "completion."

2

Transformers & LLMs from scratch

≈ 4–8 weeks Build and train a GPT from a blank file; understand every component. This is the core of modern model research — the difference between "I fine-tuned a model" and "I can reason about why it behaves as it does."

What to learn

  • Self-attention — Q/K/V, multi-head, causal masking. The centerpiece — implement it from scratch.
  • The transformer block — attention + MLP, residuals, LayerNorm/RMSNorm.
  • Tokenization — BPE; how the tokenizer shapes everything downstream (an underrated source of bugs).
  • Positional info — learned vs RoPE; why attention needs it (it's permutation-equivariant without it).
  • The full pipeline — pretrain → SFT → eval, end to end, once.

Primary path

Karpathy's "Let's build GPT" + nanoGPT (attention from a blank file). Sebastian Raschka — Build an LLM (From Scratch) as the deeper companion. nanochat to run the whole modern stack once (the resulting model is weak — running the pipeline is the point).

Deliverable — a from-scratch GPT plus one question answered empirically (e.g. "how does final loss change with depth at fixed params?"). That shift from building to investigating is what makes this Phase 2.

Milestone

  • Implement multi-head causal self-attention from a blank file; explain every line.
  • Explain why attention needs positional info and how RoPE provides it.
  • Describe what a tokenizer does + one way it can hurt quality.
  • Train a GPT end-to-end; reproduce nanochat's pipeline once.
Traps — treating attention as a formula to memorize (implement + visualize it); skipping the tokenizer; stopping at "it trains" instead of "a question answered."

3

Training & systems

≈ 8–12 weeks The longest, deepest phase — where your "massive gap" most closes. Learn how real models are trained efficiently, then reproduce a published result and run one clean ablation. This is your L1→L2 transition.

What to learn

  • Scaling laws — Chinchilla: ~20 tokens/param compute-optimal. The basis for "small but well-trained."
  • Efficiency / GPU systems — bf16, MFU, FlashAttention, a reading-level grasp of kernels (Triton) + parallelism. Know where time and memory go.
  • Data — curation, filtering, dedup. Often beats architecture; the highest-leverage, least-glamorous variable.
  • Optimization at scale — LR schedules, warmup, AdamW, Muon, gradient accumulation.
  • The small-models toolkit — quantization (GPTQ/AWQ), distillation, pruning, efficient architectures.

Primary path

Stanford CS336 — Language Modeling from Scratch (Spring 2026) — the flagship. Free lectures + public assignments: tokenizer, FlashAttention2 in Triton, distributed training, Common-Crawl data, SFT+RL. Do the assignments. Plus modded-nanogpt (read the commit history like a textbook).

Deliverable — the important one — reproduce a result on one GPU, then run ONE clean ablation (e.g. Muon vs AdamW) with ≥3 seeds + a same-size baseline. Ship repo + eval harness + plot + writeup. This artifact is your L2 credential. Pressure-test the design with the research-buddy first.

Milestone

  • Explain Chinchilla, MFU; estimate train cost on your hardware.
  • Read a profiler trace and locate the bottleneck (compute/memory/IO).
  • Implement or clearly explain FlashAttention's idea (IO-aware exact attention).
  • Reproduce a published result on one GPU + a controlled ablation.
Traps — lectures without assignments; chasing the 8×H100 speedrun record; confounded ablations (vary one thing, match the baseline, seed it); under-weighting data.

4

Post-training & evaluation

≈ 4–8 weeks How a raw pretrained model becomes useful — and how to measure it without fooling yourself. Most applied model research today is post-training + eval, and evaluation is the most under-respected skill in the field.

What to learn

  • SFT / instruction tuning — + LoRA/QLoRA for doing it cheaply on one GPU.
  • Preference & RL — RLHF, DPO (no separate reward model), GRPO/RLVR (RL from verifiable rewards — the reasoning frontier; DeepSeek's method, drops the value critic).
  • Reward modeling — reward hacking; why a verifier with skin in the game can't be fair.
  • Evaluation as a discipline — contamination, prompt sensitivity, metric≠behavior, comparative > absolute. The most important sub-topic here.

Primary path

Nathan Lambert — The RLHF Book (free online) is your spine: the canonical recipe, DPO, the RLVR renaissance, reward modeling, eval. Hugging Face TRL to actually post-train a small model.

Deliverable — post-train a 0.5–1.5B model (SFT+LoRA → DPO or a small GRPO with a verifiable reward) and evaluate it honestly: clean harness, same-size baseline, a stated way your reward could be gamed and what the metric misses.

Milestone

  • Explain SFT vs DPO vs GRPO — what each optimizes, when to use which.
  • Describe reward hacking with a concrete example.
  • Name ≥3 ways an eval can lie.
  • Post-train a small model with TRL + an honest evaluation with a baseline.
Traps — trusting your own numbers (assume the eval lies until checked); reaching for GRPO first (earn it with SFT+DPO); self-evaluation; metric tunnel-vision.

Specialization & doing research

ongoing Stop following a curriculum and start doing research: pick a niche, produce your first original result, engage a community, ship it publicly. The shift — from being assigned a task to choosing your own — is the definition of a researcher.

1 · Pick a niche (go deep, not wide)

Choose one where you can build a real result on one GPU: efficiency (quant/distill/prune), mechanistic interpretability (small models are interpretable; the community rewards small clean results — see ARENA + TransformerLens), data curation, or small reasoning / post-training. Your on-ramp doc is the detailed specialization guide.

2 · Produce your first original result

  • Reproduce something in your niche (the launchpad now, not the goal).
  • Find the open thread — the cheap ablation the paper didn't run. Reading deeply is idea generation.
  • Scope it with the research-buddy: prior-art + feasibility + confound.
  • Run it cleanly — one axis, ≥3 seeds, same-size baseline.
  • Ship the artifact — open weights/code + reproducible eval + honest baselines + writeup. Your L3 credential.

3 · Get visible & find your people

Open-source-as-research is the modern credential. EleutherAI (#research + the SOAR program) is the highest-ROI single move — collaborators, granted compute, and the arXiv-endorsement gate, solved at once. Realistic venues: the ICLR Blog Posts track, NeurIPS ENLSP workshop, the ML Reproducibility Challenge.

Milestone — you're operating as a researcher (L3) when…

  • You've shipped an original result with open code + honest eval, publicly.
  • Someone you don't know has used/cited/built on it.
  • You're active in a research community.
  • You can read a frontier paper and immediately see the next experiment.
  • You choose your own questions.
Traps — niche-hopping (depth compounds; breadth doesn't); reaching for novelty too early (your first "original" is a clean extension); building tools instead of doing research (yes — including over-investing in the research-buddy); isolation.

Research method — the craft

Learning ML and being a researcher are different skills. This is the second one: how to read, reproduce, experiment, write, and not fool yourself. Start it in week 1.

The research loop: question → hypothesis → minimal experiment → result → interpret skeptically → write it down → next question. Keep each turn small. Iteration speed — not raw intelligence — separates productive researchers from stuck ones.

Reading papers

Multi-pass: skim (figures + results, 5 min) → method + baseline (15–30) → deep only for the few that matter. Always write a 3-sentence note. Read with the right question: not "is this true?" but "how was this measured, and how could it be wrong?" — that's most of taste. 2–3/week, forever.

Reproduction

Where most learning + credibility come from — and a valued contribution in its own right. Reproduce before you extend. Containerize (Docker/uv). When your numbers don't match the paper, that gap is the most educational thing in the process.

Experiment design

One independent variable. The right (same-size) baseline. ≥3 seeds. Ablate with/without the component. Control confounds (match params and FLOPs; disjoint calibration/eval). Pre-register what would confirm/refute — before you run it.

The research log

A dated LOG.md: what I tried, what happened, what I learned, what's next. Your external memory and the raw material of every writeup. Start Phase 0; never skip.

Writing

Unwritten research barely exists. Write to find your errors — if you can't write it clearly, you don't understand it. State limitations honestly; it builds more credibility than overclaiming. Teach to learn.

Feedback & taste

Seek harsh feedback early — the person who finds your confound is doing you a favor. Adversarially self-review: before believing a result, try to refute it. Taste is pattern-matching built by volume; you can't shortcut it, only run the loop faster.

The honest meta-point — in building this very project, the assistant confidently called a real API "hallucinated" — false, caught only because someone pushed back and actually checked. That's the whole discipline in one anecdote: confidence is not evidence; verify, and prefer being corrected to being wrong.
